## In this session

Americans are wary of AI. They are less trusting of their government to regulate it than people almost anywhere else, and they are far less optimistic than AI experts. We ask where these views come from: material interests beyond your job, values, beliefs about what others think, stories and the last tech revolution, and cues from political elites. In a short activity, you predict what explains Californians' views of AI, then compare with real survey results. We then ask how opinion becomes pressure. Do AI harms raise demand for regulation? What happens when public comment can be manufactured? Can AI help or hurt democratic deliberation? Next Monday turns to persuasion, microtargeting and synthetic media.

## Questions we'll tackle

- What do Americans think about AI now, and how do partisan patterns appear to be shifting?
- Where does public opinion come from: preferences, attitudes or beliefs? How much is material interest, and how much is values, beliefs about others and elite cues?
- What can a survey result tell us, and what would make it misleading?
- Do AI-caused harms raise demand for regulation, and what evidence do we have so far?
- Can AI strengthen democratic deliberation, or does it weaken the channels through which citizens are heard?

## After class

### Key takeaways
<!-- revise after 2026-10-12 lecture -->

- **Americans lean wary.** In Pew's 2026 survey, 52% were more concerned than excited about AI in daily life. In a separate Gallup question, 39% said AI does more harm than good. Only 31% of Americans trust their government to regulate AI, the lowest share in the AI Index comparison. AI experts are far more positive than the public: 56% versus 17% expect a positive impact on the U.S. over 20 years.
- **Partisan patterns are moving.** Bloom and Makridis find Democrats report more frequent AI use at work than Republicans (30.1% versus 25% in early 2026), but that gap mostly reflects education and occupation. Perceived risk of losing one's job is different: 19.7% of Democratic workers versus 15.5% of Republican workers in early 2026. In Pew's data, Democrats are now more concerned about AI than Republicans (56% versus 49%), a reversal from 2021.
- **Opinion has several building blocks.** Preferences are what you want, attitudes are how you feel, and beliefs are what you expect. Interests reach beyond your job, and values and second-order beliefs (what you think others think) matter too. Measuring opinion is hard: the options a survey offers can change the answer, and only a randomized design shows by how much.
- **Associations, not causes.** In the PPIC November 2024 California survey, having heard more about AI and expecting one's children to be better off were associated with a more favorable view of AI. Registered party was not, once the other factors were included.
- **Do harms raise demand for rules? The evidence is still thin.**
  - In Hoes and Gilardi's experiments, existential-risk headlines raised concern but did not move support for AI policy.
  - Grant, Green and Evans find that British workers exposed to AI support stronger regulation.
  - We have not found a published experiment linking a real AI incident to support for regulation.
- **AI changes the channels between citizens and government.**
  - Public comment can be manufactured: an alleged AI-generated campaign that the company denies.
  - In the Habermas Machine experiment, participants preferred AI-drafted group statements 56% of the time. The comparison "mediators" were randomly assigned participants, not professionals.
  - Critics warn that treating deliberation's problems as technical ones depoliticizes it.
