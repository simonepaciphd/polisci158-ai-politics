## In this session

Governments are now buyers and users of generative AI, not only its regulators. We ask who gains and who loses when agencies adopt it: tech-savvy agencies gain capacity, vendors gain revenue, street-level bureaucrats may lose discretion, and voters and benefit recipients may lose accountability, while automated decisions can scale a single failure across thousands of people. We then turn to the state as regulator and ask why a general-purpose, fast-moving technology that firms understand better than governments is so hard to govern, and which tools regulators have.

## Questions we'll tackle

- Who gains and who loses from AI in government: agencies, vendors, bureaucrats, or voters and recipients?
- How can automated decisions scale failures, and where in a public process should a human stay in the loop?
- What makes AI-governance different from e-governance?
- Why is AI so hard to regulate, and which regulatory tool (standards and guidance, audits and red-teaming, liability, licensing, disclosure) fits which problem?

## After class

### Key takeaways
<!-- revise after 2026-10-28 lecture -->

- AI in government redistributes: capacity toward tech-savvy agencies, revenue toward vendors, and discretion away from street-level bureaucrats, with accountability to voters and recipients at stake.
- Automated decisions can scale failures. Michigan's MiDAS system decided 40,195 fraud cases by computer alone, and 85 percent of those findings were reversed on review.
- Governments adopt AI against a backdrop of low trust in government and a wide gap between experts and the public: 56% of AI experts but only 17% of U.S. adults expect AI to have a positive effect on the country over the next 20 years.
- AI is hard to regulate because it is a general-purpose technology, it changes faster than rules (the Collingridge dilemma: control is easiest when we know least), firms know more than governments about capabilities, risks, and usage, and evaluations cannot prove a risk is absent.
- Regulators have several tools (standards and guidance, audits and red-teaming, liability, licensing, disclosure), and each fits some harms better than others.
