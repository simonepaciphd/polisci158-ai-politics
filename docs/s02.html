In this session

We start from a simple political economy recipe: workers exposed to a new technology lose, everyone else enjoys lower prices, and their politics follows. With AI, “exposure” can mean displacement (jobs automated away) or reinstatement (jobs made more productive), and our best measures disagree most about the most exposed jobs. The newest payroll evidence finds no economy-wide job loss so far, but falling employment for young workers in AI-exposed occupations, mostly through less hiring. We then ask when job losses turn into political action, using the China trade shock as a benchmark, and what compensation and organized labor can do for the workers who lose.

Questions we’ll tackle

  • If your exposure is your politics, what does it mean to be “exposed” to AI: substitution or augmentation?
  • Why do AI exposure indexes disagree most about the most exposed jobs, and can we trust layoff announcements instead?
  • Early-career workers in AI-exposed jobs are being hired less, not fired more. Who notices a job that was never offered, and can that loss be mobilized politically?
  • Will AI’s productivity gains show up in workers’ pay?
  • When do we expect a stronger political response: smaller, concentrated job shocks or larger, economy-wide shocks?
  • Does mass sacrifice bring mass compensation, and what role can organized labor play?

Before class

Read in this order

  • Priority: Labor Market AI Exposure: What Do We Know?, Martha Gimbel, Joshua Kendall, and Ryan Kulsakdinun, The Budget Lab at Yale (2026). Read first: how several AI exposure measures compare, and where they disagree.
  • Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence, Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar, and Ruyu Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab (2025; revised August 2026). Payroll data on millions of U.S. workers: read the six facts, and notice how carefully the authors separate description from causation.
  • The China Shock: Learning from Labor-Market Adjustment to Large Changes in Trade, David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson (2016), Annual Review of Economics. Read for how slowly local labor markets adjusted to a large job shock, and how little of the lost income government transfers replaced.

Listen or watch

  • The New Jobs AI Will Create, The AI Daily Brief, Nathaniel Whittemore (May 10, 2026), 31 min. The case for reinstatement: how cheaper AI-enabled services could create demand for new kinds of human work.

Explore

  • AI Economic Indicators, Stanford Digital Economy Lab (ongoing, updated monthly). The Canaries Dashboard extends the paper above with monthly payroll data; the other dashboards track AI adoption and macroeconomic signals. Pick one and note what it can and cannot tell you.
  • California AI-Unemployment Tracker, California Policy Lab and California Employment Development Department (2026). Unemployment insurance claims by AI exposure, education, and region; compare the statewide picture with the Bay Area.
  • JobsLost.ai (2026). A tracker of AI-attributed layoffs compiled from company announcements and news reports; ask what counting announcements misses.
  • The Productivity–Pay Gap, Economic Policy Institute (data 1948–2026). Productivity and typical workers’ pay grew together until the late 1970s and then diverged; ask whether AI would change that pattern.

After class

Key takeaways

  • The baseline model is material self-interest: exposed workers lose jobs or wages and oppose the change, while those who are not exposed enjoy lower prices. The same recipe has been used for trade and globalization.
  • AI exposure cuts two ways. Full automation of a job substitutes for workers (fewer jobs, lower wages); partial automation can augment them (more jobs, higher productivity and wages). Which one dominates is an open empirical question.
  • Measurement is hard. Exposure indexes disagree more about highly exposed jobs, layoff announcements are noisy, and California’s tracker finds no statewide surge in AI-related claims but rising claims among college-educated workers in high-exposure jobs and in the Bay Area.
  • The Stanford Digital Economy Lab’s payroll data show no economy-wide displacement through June 2026, but employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with their less-exposed peers. The gap comes mainly from less hiring, not more firing, and it is concentrated where AI is used to automate rather than complement work. The authors present these as descriptive facts, not causal estimates.
  • Preferences alone do not pass policy; job losses need mobilization to produce a response. After the China shock, each $1,000 of import exposure per worker cut annual household wage and salary income per working-age adult by about $549, while government transfers rose by only about $58 per person, just $3.65 of it through unemployment insurance and Trade Adjustment Assistance.
  • Historically, large compensation followed mass sacrifice. Organized labor is one channel for AI: the Writers Guild won AI protections in its 2023 contract, and public approval of unions is high, but union membership is at a historic low.

Go further

  • What Determines Individual Trade-Policy Preferences?, Kenneth Scheve and Matthew Slaughter (2001), Journal of International Economics. The classic “your exposure is your politics” study, applied to trade; ask how well it carries over to AI.
  • The China Syndrome: Local Labor Market Effects of Import Competition in the United States, David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson (2013), American Economic Review. The original China shock paper, including the rise in transfer payments in exposed areas.
  • On the Persistence of the China Shock, David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson (2021), Brookings Papers on Economic Activity. Whether exposed places recovered a decade later.
  • Places versus People: The Ins and Outs of Labor Market Adjustment to Globalization, David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson, Maggie Jones, and Bradley Setzler (2025), NBER working paper. Why exposed places can rebound while exposed workers do not.
  • Tracking AI-Related Job Loss Using Unemployment Insurance Claims Data in California, Ben Hyman, Till von Wachter, Diana Herrera, Roozbeh Moghadam, Jacob Morris, Swapnil Motghare, and Kara Segal, California Policy Lab (2026). The report behind the tracker’s charts.
  • GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models, Tyna Eloundou, Sam Manning, Pamela Mishkin, and Daniel Rock (2023), open working-paper version of their 2024 Science article. One of the exposure measures compared in the Budget Lab chart and used by the California tracker and the Canaries paper.
  • Which Economic Tasks are Performed with AI? Evidence from Millions of Claude Conversations, Kunal Handa, Alex Tamkin, Miles McCain, Saffron Huang, Esin Durmus, et al. (2025), Anthropic Economic Index. Measures observed AI use rather than potential exposure; the Canaries paper uses its automation and augmentation split.
  • Taxing the Rich: A History of Fiscal Fairness in the United States and Europe, Kenneth Scheve and David Stasavage (2016). Why countries taxed the rich heavily after mass mobilization for war.
  • Historical Highest Marginal Income Tax Rates, Tax Policy Center. The data behind the top-tax-rate chart, 1913 onward.
  • Artificial Intelligence: Know Your Rights, Writers Guild of America West (updated 2025). The AI provisions writers won in the 2023 contract.
  • Labor Union Approval Relatively Steady at 68% in U.S., Megan Brenan, Gallup (2025). Union approval since 1936.
  • Six Facts About Union Density in the United States, Lauren Bauer and Tia Cole, The Hamilton Project (2026). Union membership in the public and private sectors, 1985 to 2025.
